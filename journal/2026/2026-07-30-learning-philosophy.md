# Engineering Journal — A Change in How I Learn

**Date:** 30 July 2026

Today I realised that the biggest thing holding me back wasn't the difficulty of the subjects or the lack of resources—it was my approach to learning.

When I started college, my plan was to build a strong theoretical foundation before attempting any meaningful projects. I believed I needed to understand every concept thoroughly before writing code, designing hardware, or starting a project. Looking back, I now realise I was chasing perfection instead of progress.

Over the past few weeks, I spent a significant amount of time reading textbooks and trying to learn topics from the ground up. While the information was valuable, much of it went far deeper than what I actually needed at this stage. I was learning like a researcher when my current goal is to become a builder.

Today I also recognised something important about myself.

I am a **project-first learner**.

This is how I naturally learned Python. I never mastered the language by reading a book cover to cover. Instead, I picked a project, watched someone build it, understood the ideas behind each step, experimented on my own, made mistakes, searched for answers, and gradually built a solid foundation. Every project introduced a few new concepts, and over time those concepts accumulated into real understanding.

Some of the best engineers seem to follow a similar pattern. They don't wait until they know everything before they begin. They build, encounter problems, learn what they need, and continue building. The project becomes the roadmap for learning.

The mistake I made was trying to apply a theory-first approach to a goal that is fundamentally project-driven.

My long-term goal is ambitious enough that trying to understand everything before starting is impossible. There will always be another book to read, another chapter to finish, or another concept to master. If I continue waiting until I "know enough," I may never start.

From today onward, my learning philosophy changes.

I will build first and learn what each project demands.

That does **not** mean ignoring theory. It means learning theory when it becomes useful. Instead of studying entire books in advance, I will study the concepts required to complete the next milestone. This keeps learning focused, practical, and directly connected to building.

My projects should also follow a progression.

Rather than jumping directly into building an AI accelerator or a custom processor, I will work through increasingly challenging projects. Each project should introduce a few new ideas while reinforcing everything I've already learned. Every completed project becomes another stepping stone towards the larger vision.

Another important lesson is that unfinished perfection is less valuable than completed imperfection.

An imperfect project teaches far more than a perfectly highlighted textbook.

From now on, success will not be measured by how many chapters I have read. It will be measured by what I have built, documented, understood, and improved.

This isn't a change in my destination.

It's a change in the path I will take to get there.

And I believe this path suits the kind of engineer I want to become.
